## What constitutes a lender-ready deal

**Simon:** I want to test the idea of going to a lender with a deal in hand. What information does a VFI BDO need on initial outreach?

**Stauss:** It depends on lender size. Smaller-ticket lenders ask for different things and may run personal credit. VFI doesn't run personal FICO, though it may require a personal guarantee.

For VFI's initial review, I always request:

1. The last two full years of financial statements — currently 2024 and 2025.
2. The most recently completed 2026 interim period — ideally July 31, otherwise June 30.
3. A year-over-year comparable for the same interim period — June 2026 versus June 2025, for example — to see the trend.
4. A detailed use-of-funds or equipment list: the total capital ask, what they're buying, whether it's new or used, and the relevant components.

For a $5 million truck-and-trailer deal, I need to know whether it also includes GPS units, deer guards, and refrigeration retrofits. I don't always need every purchase order initially; I need enough detail to explain the equipment and use of funds to my team.

That package is standard for middle-market, upper-middle-market, and larger companies. Ideally the full-year statements are audited or reviewed. It's enough for me to say whether my team has an appetite. Later I may ask for a full asset list if I need additional collateral.

If Quintel starts packaging deals for lenders and taking a success fee, there is more to build: agreements defining when and how Quintel is paid, loan-versus-lease differences, and fee negotiations. A point is standard for me because I don't want smaller deals, but a $1.5 million deal might support two or three points.

There is also the question of expansion. A broker may bring a $2 million equipment deal, but after we speak with the client we find a $10 million opportunity through additional assets or a sale-leaseback. The agreement must define whether the broker is paid on the amount brought in or the full expanded deal.

Quintel's positioning could be: lenders already pay for the software, and Quintel accepts a lower success fee — perhaps 50 basis points — on every funded deal it brings. It could prioritize volume and quality over maximizing points.

**Stauss:** Smaller-ticket lenders require more: tax returns, three to six months of bank statements, an owner's credit application authorizing a FICO pull, and usually a personal guarantee. The package depends on the company size and the lender.

Quintel could rank lenders by execution quality, not just fee. I would rather place a one-point deal with a lender that consistently approves and funds than a two-point deal with one that doesn't get things done.

If you're brokering through Onset, you're dependent on how well they place the deal with their bank or funding source. VFI does everything in-house, so it can move faster on credit decisions and mid-deal changes. When onboarding a lender, Quintel needs to know where its money comes from: self-funded, investor-backed, bank-backed, a white-label servicer that sells paper to a bank, or a syndicate. How the lender sources funds is central to its real execution capability.

## Milestone economics versus lead-sharing

**Aleksandar:** We're deciding whether to broker the deals the platform surfaces, or let lenders such as Onset, Wingspire, and VFI work them and earn a smaller percentage.

**Stauss:** I would like a separate session on that. I have a different philosophy: take a smaller share of the success fee but collect milestone payments along the way so some revenue is more certain.

Suppose Quintel identifies VFI as a top-tier partner. You might require a lender to be a software client to receive deal flow. Then you say: we're going to bring highly qualified deals and only charge half a point on successful funding, below the usual one or two points.

But add milestone payments. If you bring a $5 million deal and the client executes VFI's term sheet, Quintel earns a fee. If VFI approves it, Quintel earns another fee. If it funds, Quintel earns the 50-basis-point success fee. The exact numbers could be $1,000 on execution and $2,000 on approval, or larger; the principle is that Quintel shouldn't receive nothing if it originated a qualified opportunity but VFI's documentation team loses it at the eleventh hour.

The lender may ultimately pay less than a standard referral fee, but Quintel gets revenue for producing quality through the funnel. The agreement could include SLAs: if Quintel misses the agreed quality or conversion targets, it only earns the success fee.

**Aleksandar:** Is it realistic for VFI to make Quintel a preferred partner and route most of its mid/high-ticket volume through it?

**Stauss:** Don't focus on high-ticket; focus on credit-qualified for VFI's profile. Position Quintel as an extension of the origination team and hold it accountable to funnel metrics. For example: send 20 deals; VFI issues term sheets on 15; 10 get executed; eight get approved and funded.

Benchmark that against the average of VFI's top 10 sales reps. If Quintel produces like a top rep, compare its economics with a rep earning a $50–70K base plus commission, perhaps $300–400K total. Quintel can reduce the success commission while tying most compensation to lender success, but still get paid for producing qualified deals. Origination shouldn't be accountable for the lender's documentation or underwriting failures.

That model doesn't fit smaller-ticket lending as well. Small-ticket is rate-sheet and algorithm driven: personal credit, payment history, debt-to-income, DSCR, then approve or decline. There, Quintel should probably take two or three points per funded deal. Decisions and funding happen much faster.

**Aleksandar:** Is bringing complete deals more attractive to VFI than giving all VFI reps access to Quintel leads and taking commission on anything they fund?

**Stauss:** The lead-sharing model wouldn't work for VFI. VFI would say its database is 38 years older than yours. Quintel may be more technical and source unique information, but VFI already has a proprietary lead database. VFI won't revenue-share simply because Quintel says an account looks promising and a VFI rep still has to contact it, collect documents, and do all the work.

The value threshold is a full package on a private company plus a direct client relationship: the financials, the use of funds, and an open line to schedule an introduction with the client that week. Without that, there isn't enough incremental value for a success fee.

**Aleksandar:** So the full package is the threshold.

**Stauss:** The full package and an open line. Quintel could market directly to CFOs as an AI-curated financing advisor, or onboard brokers who upload active deals and then match those deals to the lenders most likely to approve and close them. A shared process could let a broker upload a deal, Quintel verify it, VFI qualify it, and the borrower book a call. Quintel could split the fee with the broker.

There may also be a data-driven model where Quintel supplies hotter leads while the lender does most of the work, but you need to ask what economics actually fit. Other lenders may have a different philosophy from VFI.
