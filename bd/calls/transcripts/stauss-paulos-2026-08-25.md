**Alek:** At some point that begins to look like brokerage. If Quintel finds the lead and does the outreach, why not take a clip on the deal?

**Stauss:** The effort is real, and deals that look excellent can fail late. We are working a $23M GPU/data-center transaction for a company with audited financials, roughly $150M revenue, related guarantors, and a principal guaranteeing with unencumbered commercial real estate. We obtained executed documents, but the vendor does not want the client named on the invoice. That creates enough diligence concern to put the deal at risk. Even strong middle-market debt deals are not automatic.

I would not rely on a success fee alone. So far this year VFI has sent term sheets on **$2.2B**, received executed term sheets on **$800M**, and has formal approvals just under **$300M**. Some of that $2.2B was marginal, but the fall-off is the point: a deal sheet is not a funded deal.

There is value in progress-based payments and in Quintel helping the deal move. If it is two days after the client received a document request, Quintel can send a factual reminder: “To preserve the August 28 funding timeline, we need the executed document by end of day.” That is different from the lender sounding desperate or pressuring the borrower.

**Alek:** We are onboarding Providence Capital. Quintel will receive 25% of gross margin on funded deals. We are also working toward a similar arrangement with Wingspire.

**Stauss:** That can be more valuable than a conventional point fee. But if Quintel shares materially in a $25M deal, it should help protect the outcome: request tracking, documentation, e-signature reminders, timeline updates, and next-best actions. It becomes a white-labeled sales/deal-execution layer for the lender.

The account can become a host of agents: after credit approval, identify remaining documents, know who to contact and copy, communicate funding-timeline consequences, and give the CFO clear updates. This reduces human gaps when someone gets pulled into another issue.

**Simon:** That is comparable to AI transaction coordination in real estate: a checklist across parties that pushes forms and dates forward.

**Stauss:** Correct, but each transaction has exceptions. A Canadian security-camera installation on a barge required outside counsel because of Canadian law, water jurisdiction, attachment to a leased barge, and title/landlord-waiver issues. Quintel should not give legal advice, but it could identify the issue, update the document checklist, explain the expected delay, and tell the borrower what VFI is doing.

There is also fragmentation in capital structure, not just documentation. An approval can expire as a company deteriorates. A borrower who uses its revolver to buy planes may lose capacity to acquire an adjacent company, then scramble for a sale-leaseback. A CFO should maintain complementary relationships: operating bank and deposits, revolver for opportunistic liquidity, ABL where applicable, and dedicated equipment finance.

On the borrower side, a free Quintel service could help a CFO map a capital stack after a warehouse-expansion signal: real estate, ABL, hard equipment, soft costs, racking, HVAC, technology, trucks, and automation. The immediate use is showing that some lenders can finance 100% of hard and soft costs rather than forcing cash or revolver use.
