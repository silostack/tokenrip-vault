## Key Intelligence / What Changed

### 1. Rob handed us the ZoomInfo argument, verbatim, and it was not taken

> **Rob:** *"We built intent signals around ZoomInfo, and sometimes those are too late to the party... Sometimes somebody somewhere clicked on something and it looks like they're buying equipment. **They're not even equipment intensive.**"*

That is the tear sheet §3.5 differentiation — browsing-behaviour inference is late and wrong — spoken **by the buyer, unprompted, as a complaint about a tool he already pays for.** It is also the closest thing to a pain voice on this call.

Simon's entire response was *"Yes, yes, we know it very well."* Then Rob changed the subject to Spencer.

The rehearsed answer was in the prep sheet and in the playbook. This is the fourth time in the log a buyer has raised ZoomInfo, and the first time one has raised it as a *complaint about their own stack* rather than a comparison — the easiest version of the objection we will ever get, and it went unclaimed. **Put it in the follow-up email verbatim:** *"You said their signals are sometimes too late and sometimes not even equipment-intensive. That's structural — ZoomInfo reads browsing behaviour matched to a company by IP. We read the public record: a filing, an award, a permit. That's not an inference, it's an event."*

### 2. The make-vs-buy objection was in the room, wearing a headset, and was never spoken to

> **Rob:** *"The reason I brought in our VP of technology is he's working with Claude to build accounts that should be targeted. He's building Claude to let guys run their own spreads instead of waiting three days to get them back from our credit department. **So I don't know if he's already working on something like this**, or questions he would have."*

Rob stated the purpose of Spencer's presence out loud: to tell him *"I'm already running that."* Spencer then sat through the entire demo in silence. Simon invited questions once (*"feel free to grill me"*) and never addressed him again.

**This is the fifth make-vs-buy instance in the log and by far the most consequential** — the others were reported secondhand (TFG's analysts, Capteris's intern, FS Growth's raise-tracker). Here the builder was live and reachable. Two things follow:

- Spencer's silence is not agreement. It is an unread verdict, and he will deliver it to Rob and Eric in a room we are not in — the exact champion→buyer failure the vault has now logged four times.
- The second half of what Spencer is building — *reps running their own spreads instead of waiting three days on credit* — is **not sourcing at all.** It is the packaging/underwriting module. A credit bottleneck that makes reps wait three days is a named, quantified internal pain at a $700M lender, and it went completely unexplored.

### 3. Rob described their sourcing honestly, and it is not what the prep predicted

> *"When you go after private equity, you're able to usually get in with private equity. You see their platforms and then you try to make relationships there... I got a lead today, I'm talking to [firm] because I've been doing deals with them for six years, for referral sources. But a lot of it is, sometimes it's **call and hope**, right? You just look at a website."*

And then, decisively:

**This overturns the prep's primary angle.** The call sheet argued sponsor-side intelligence was the wedge. Rob says the opposite: sponsor coverage is the part that already works, because portfolios are published. The gap is everything *outside* the sponsor network — where his reps are doing "call and hope" off websites and an employee-size/revenue/equipment-intensity model. **That is exactly the standard product**, at a $5–50M ticket.

Good news for fit; a correction for the pitch. Lead the follow-up with the non-sponsor gap, not with sponsor watching.

### 4. 🔴 The Katharine / Empire disclosure — the most serious item on this call

Describing an anonymous customer's problem, Simon volunteered enough to identify her, then confirmed the first name under a direct probe:

> **Rob:** *"Was she in New York?"* → **Simon:** *"I believe so, yeah."*
> **Rob:** *"You talking to [name]?"* → **Simon:** *"No, no, no. **This was Catherine.**"*
> **Rob:** *"I wonder if it's that new girl, Empire."*
> **Simon:** *"The Empire rings a bell. **We haven't spoken to Empire**, so I'm not going to say whether you're right or not."*
> **Rob:** *"Yeah, **I think I know who she is.**"*

Three separate problems, in ascending order:

1. **A named prospect was described to a peer institution** — role, ticket profile, list size, and first name. Katharine Rudzitis at Empire Asset Finance is a live deal in our own pipeline (6/18, strongest first-call pull in the log).

The prep sheet for this call warned about it in the opposite direction, and VFI was correctly protected. Empire wasn't, because Empire wasn't in the warning.

**The rule this produces, which now belongs in the tear sheet:** customer anecdotes are told with the *mechanic* and nothing else — *"one lender we work with runs a watchlist over a thousand lines long"* — and when a buyer guesses, the answer is always the same sentence, whether they are right or wrong: **"I don't confirm or deny who we work with, and that's the same protection you'd get."** That answer is true, it costs nothing, and it sells trust instead of spending it.

### Coaching Priorities

**1. Never describe a named customer to a peer institution — and never say something untrue to buy your way out of it.**
Rob correctly identified Katharine at Empire from Simon's own detail, and the exit line (*"we haven't spoken to Empire"*) was false and delivered to someone who already knew.
→ **Better language, for the anecdote:** *"One lender we work with keeps a watchlist over a thousand lines long and needs the portfolio watched rather than the list re-read."* No geography, no gender, no name.
→ **Better language, when a buyer guesses:** *"I don't confirm or deny who we work with — and that's exactly the protection you'd get."* Say it identically whether the guess is right or wrong. That is the only version that stays true and it converts an awkward moment into a trust signal.
→ **Why it matters:** Rob competes with Empire. The specific thing he now believes he knows is that a direct competitor is buying our product. And the untruth is the part that cannot be walked back casually — a buyer who later works out he was told something false stops believing the accuracy claims too.

**2. The technical evaluator was in the room to say "we're already doing that," and was never asked anything.**
Rob announced Spencer's purpose out loud. Spencer sat silent through the entire demo.
→ **Better language:** *"Spencer, before I go further — Rob says you're building some of this with Claude. What's it covering today, and where does it break? I'd genuinely rather find the overlap now than sell Rob something you're already halfway through."*
→ **Why it matters:** silence from the technical evaluator is not consent, it is a verdict we haven't heard, and it gets delivered to Eric without us. Five make-vs-buy instances in six weeks; this was the only one where the builder was reachable in real time.

**3. Answered a packaging question with a price cut, and disclosed the wrong band's number doing it.**
*"Is there anything less than that, like a thousand dollar one seat?"* → *"our smaller ticket pricing is 1,500... I'm sure there's something we could work out."*
→ **Better language:** *"The band keys on average ticket, not seat count — at 5 to 50 million you're on the $3,000 plan whether one rep uses it or three. And one rep is the worst possible test: he gets busy for two weeks and you've learned nothing. Your three grinders is the real test."*
→ **Why it matters:** he was probing packaging, not resisting price — he had just said the number was fine. The reply gave him a lower anchor, a signal that price is soft, and a reason to buy the smaller thing. Fourth self-initiated concession in four weeks, all four volunteered before being pushed.

*(Minor, recurring:)* **The 30-day money-back term is not on the ratified card** and was invented live — and the buyer rejected it as gimmicky. **No date was requested** at any point. **"I'll send the deck"** commits to an artifact that does not exist.
