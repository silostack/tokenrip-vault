## Key Intelligence / What Changed

**1. Providence is at break-even, and the owners' money is five years out. This is the ceiling on everything we can charge.**
August: $758K total GM revenue → 50% to AEs and Max → ~$375K left → ~$200K+ monthly opex before utilities → break-even around $300K/month. "When you get to $758,000, that means it was a break-even. Nobody made any money. Owners didn't make any money. **They make their money five years down the road.**" The front/back split has moved from 70-30 to **40-60** in two and a half years — 60% of owner economics now comes from end-of-lease 3/5/10% buyouts and residuals, not from current-period commission. Revenue is "flat or declining" while opex has tripled (operations 5 → 17 people, plus PayNet at $25/pull against a 50-applications-to-5-approvals ratio). Plus a loss reserve Max doesn't see: on a repurchase, the funding source claws back the commission, and two full-time people do nothing but collection.

**So what:** there is almost no current cash in this business to share. Any rev-share number we name is measured against a $375K pre-opex pool that is already underwater. This is the substrate under David's next two points.

**2. The Max rev-share is dead, and David materially walked back his own 09-10 anchor.**
Verbatim: "**This rev sharing has not been approved by Providence. This is Max's.**" And: "if I'm the owner of a company, which I have been forever, I would never agree to that." He priced the zone precisely — **2–3% off the house's 50% is "a possibility"; 15/20/30% is "dead on arrival."** Note the delta against his own unprompted 09-10 framing of "~25% of funded value" and "~$300 CPI." On his $7,853 average, 25% is ~$1,960/deal; he is now capping at ~$1,000 and calling that generous. **Our 09-10 read of "David negotiating against himself" was wrong — or he has since checked it against reality.** Either way, treat the 25% number as retired, not as a floor.

**3. His counter-model routes around Providence ownership entirely — and that is its real value.**
"**On every deal, I have the ability as an AE to say this came as a referral from company ABC. And the company will pay a referral fee.** The referral fee hits me as much as it hits the company... it can't be more than a thousand... that thousand goes directly to you guys... Then we figure out from the 45% that I have left how much I can share with you. **And we put that in writing and we sign it.**" Closing restatement: "as far as referral fee goes, **100% to you on every funded deal**, and then we figure out how to divvy up the pie between us, and also how to share costs on infrastructure." This is a structure that needs no owner approval, has a defined trigger (funded), and comes with a named signatory who is sitting across the table. That is worth more than a larger number that requires Lee and Horn to say yes. It resolves the authority question in contact-doc assumption #4 by sidestepping it.

**Price anchors he set, for the record:** raw leads $3–4; applications ~$10, "$100 an application, who's going to pay that" — and then he argued against his own framing: "**forget the application — funded deals.** And that would be the referral fee."

**4. Vendors are 70% of the business, and the vendor ICP is now specified.**
Excluding David's own deals (he has zero vendors), **70% of August's business came from vendors.** The ICP, in his criteria: located in the outskirts and small towns, not major metros (same logic as the cheap perimeter booths at a trade show); not backed by a captive or a Wells Fargo; found via published trade-association member lists; and — the operative filter — **no lender listed on their website.** "Financing tab — you just scrape the financing tab. If it has it, and it has multiple numbers or names on it, they're out." And the target inside the vendor is not the company: "**our ICP is those salespeople, not the company**" — because their commission depends on the deal funding. Yield expectation: 2–3 deals/month from a real vendor, 5–6 from a good one.

**5. The headwind, quantified — and it directly contradicts Simon's projection.**

Against Simon's "five relationships month one, ten by month two, thirty to fifty by month six," David's answer: incumbent reps have locked up the vendors with golf, $5,000 checks and first-class tickets, they will not switch, "and **I think that population is a lot smaller than what you think it is.**" He does not know yet what we could offer to beat a Danny — "I have to figure that out." **This is the single most important unresolved risk in the vendor thesis and it is cheap to test** (see action #5).

## Internal read — is the referral model actually good for us?

**The case for taking it:** it needs no owner approval, it has a signatory in the room, it triggers on an event we can verify through our own domains, and it comes with cost-sharing and a motivated operator who will hand us live data. Compared to a 25% rev share that dies in front of Lee and Horn, a signable $1,000 is infinitely larger.

**The case against taking it as-is:** at his stated floor (2% of contacts apply, 10% of applications fund) one funded deal costs roughly 500 contacts. At $1,000 that is $2 per contact to cover sourcing, verification, domains, inboxes, sending, and build — before margin. It works only at his *owner ratios* (25-5-1), which nobody has hit, or at higher tickets, or with the 45% share meaningfully specified. **The unnamed share of his 45% is where the deal actually lives, and it went unquantified.** That is the number to pin down next call, not the cap.

**The strategic tension nobody said out loud:** David's model requires exclusivity to one lender. Quintel's model requires many lenders. He is offering a good deal that, taken literally, caps the company at one customer in EF small-ticket — with an exit path that is "become a broker yourself," which he spent ten minutes arguing is a dying business. Worth deciding deliberately rather than drifting into.
