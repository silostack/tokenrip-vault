You are evaluating a company and product strategy document for Tokenrip. Your job is not to review it. Your job is to rebuild the idea from the ground up, decide whether the founders' version is the best version, and if not, produce a better one.

Read the document once to understand it. Then put it aside and reason from first principles about the same problem before you compare.

## Who is asking

Two founders, one technical (Simon) and one on the business side (Alek), building a shared workspace that people's AI agents plug into. They have a live product, a real pilot with an outside collaborator, and no acquisition machine. They can build fast and have limited runway for motions that do not produce paying users. They want to be told where they are wrong, not reassured.

## What you are given

The document tags claims as [fact], [decision], or [inference]. Trust the facts. Treat the decisions as revisable. Attack the inferences first. Section 13 (risks), section 14 (assumptions), and section 16 (decided versus hypothesis) tell you where the founders think they are exposed; do not limit yourself to that list.

## What to do, in order

**1. Restate the idea in your own words, in one paragraph.** If you cannot, say what is unclear. If your restatement differs from the document's own one-paragraph version, that difference is a finding.

**2. Rebuild from first principles.** Take the starting conditions as given: every person is getting an always-on AI agent inside a vendor host; those hosts do not share state with each other; many counterparties have no agent or a different one. Ask what the most valuable thing to build in that world is, for whom, and how it gets its first hundred users and its first dollar. Do this without reference to Tokenrip's existing product or vocabulary. Then compare your answer to the document's. Where they differ, say which is better and why.

**3. Pressure test each part.** For each of the following, give a verdict (sound, weak, wrong, or unknowable yet) and the single strongest argument against it:

- The thesis that the person-firm boundary dissolves and the shared record is what remains
- The principal-first account model (a person with workspaces; the firm is a workspace with N above one)
- The choice of AI-native small companies as the first ICP, and the claim that the personal-agent wave grows that ICP rather than replacing it
- The two first hours (drafted decisions plus a correction; a surface with a second writer)
- Correction and second-writer as activation metrics
- Boundary expansion through outsiders as the growth mechanism
- The concierge outbound motion and its kill signals
- Per-workspace pricing with two gates (size and autopilot)
- The competitive claim that no host will hold neutral state for another host's users
- The decision to reject a consumer matching network and a consumer pivot
- Risk one, "it feels like work," and whether the proposed mitigation is sufficient

**4. Look for what is missing.** Angles the document does not consider. Second-order effects. Regulatory or trust issues. Ways the hosts could close the position. A buyer the founders have not named. A distribution channel that fits the product better than the ones listed. A sequencing that reaches revenue sooner. Name the thing that would embarrass the founders in six months if it turns out to be obvious.

**5. Propose the better version, if there is one.** If you believe a different product, ICP, first hour, or GTM would do better, write it as a concrete alternative: what to build first, who to sell it to, how the first fifty users arrive, what they pay for, and what to measure in week one. Be specific enough that it could be executed Monday. If the founders' version is the best version, say so plainly and say what you tried that lost to it.

**6. Give a decision.** One paragraph. Continue as written, continue with named changes, or change direction. Then the one assumption your decision rests on and the cheapest test that would disconfirm it within two weeks.

## Rules

- Lead with the verdict on each point. Evidence after, not before.
- Do not restate the document back. Do not summarize sections. Do not produce a list of things that are "good" unless you also say what would make them fail.
- Do not hedge everything. Where you are confident, say so. Where you are not, say what would make you confident.
- Distinguish between "this is wrong" and "this is untested." Both matter; they call for different actions.
- If you propose an alternative, hold it to the same standard: name its weakest assumption.
- No generic startup advice. Every recommendation must reference something specific in this document or in the world it describes.
- Be direct. The founders have asked to be told where they are wrong.

## Output shape

Sections numbered 1 through 6 as above. Verdicts in bold at the start of each item in section 3. Total length whatever the substance requires; a shorter answer that changes a decision beats a longer one that restates the plan.
