# Technicians: the party who sees the need before anyone else

## 1. The so what

Every intent signal in the program is a proxy for one instant: the moment a business owner who does not plan is told his unit is not worth fixing. The technician is present at that instant and often decides the replacement. The owner is the one with the problem: a $40–150K lump sum he was not planning for, so the job stalls. A financing link on the shop's quote gives the owner a monthly number on the spot and hands Providence a borrower who has already been told what he needs and what it costs. The shop's reason to pass it on is a finder's fee on funded deals and a job that closes instead of stalling; it costs him nothing. Nobody sells this to service contractors at the commercial small-ticket level today. The cheapest version is a link per shop on the existing site; the test is twenty shops in one metro.

## 2. The four scenarios, and which one is the product

What decides where we fit is who carries the ticket between the order and the payment.

| Scenario | Who fronts the money | What happens today | Where financing changes it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Small part, bill at the end** (alternator, sensor) | the shop, on distributor trade credit | nothing to finance | nowhere. Ignore |
| **Big-ticket component the tech orders and installs** (rooftop unit, walk-in rack, process chiller, standby genset, hydraulic system, truck body or upfit; $10–150K) | the owner. The shop asks for a deposit before ordering and the balance at install; the distributor's trade credit covers the shop's gap | the owner stalls, shops the quote, puts it on a card, or defers; the job slips or dies | **the seam.** A monthly number at the quote closes the job; the funder pays the shop at delivery; the owner pays monthly. The owner's lump-sum problem is solved; the shop gets a closed job and a finder's fee |
| **Tech says "buy a new one" and the owner goes to a dealer** (diesel shop, engine gone) | the dealer | the tech gets nothing; the buyer arrives at the dealer cold | tech gets a referral link; buyer arrives with a number in hand. Weak for the tech unless paid |
| **Major repair as the ticket** ($25–50K repower, transmission, rebuild) | the shop wants a deposit | owner defers or scraps the unit | repair financing. Soft-cost-heavy; most EF lenders cap soft costs at ~20–25%; Providence's position unknown. May need a different funder |

Row two is the product. Rows three and four are adjacent and cheap once row two exists.

## 3. Why this is a known playbook one market over

Residential HVAC, roofing, plumbing and solar contractors put financing on every proposal because it closes more jobs, and the contractor is paid in full at completion by the lender. Synchrony, GreenSky, Service Finance and Wisetack are the hidden lenders behind that; the contractor pays a dealer fee for promotional rates and the software (ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Jobber) embeds the offer in the proposal. It is a large, mature, contractor-distributed consumer-finance channel.

The commercial small-ticket equivalent does not exist at the contractor level. Clicklease, Approve and Currency sell point-of-sale commercial financing to *dealers*; none of them is in the service bay. The shop that orders a $60K rack from a distributor and installs it has no financing button. That is the gap.

## 4. What Quintel is in the seam

In equipment finance the vendor is whoever invoices. The contractor is the seller of record: his invoice for unit, delivery and install; the funder pays him at delivery on the owner's behalf. Three layers of value, cheapest first:

1. **The shop link.** One URL and a QR sticker per shop. The tech sends it with the quote or shows it on his phone. The owner enters the equipment and the price, sees a monthly range, gives the business name and a work email. Same site, same funnel (`funnel.md`), the shop pre-filled as seller of record, attribution baked into the link. No integration, no software sale, no new surface.
2. **The carrot for the shop.** A finder's fee on funded deals, paid by us out of the referral economics, plus a job that closes. Being paid in full at delivery by the funder is a convenience, not the reason; the shop never carried the ticket.
3. **Proposal-tool integration**, later. The financing offer inside ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Jobber, FieldEdge (HVAC), Fullbay, Shopmonkey (truck shops), the way residential financing already is. Distribution at scale once the link version proves the seam.

Two multipliers that are easy to miss:

- **The distributor sits one level up.** Ferguson, Johnstone, FleetPride and the truck-body OEMs extend the trade credit that makes the shop say "deposit first." A distributor wants its contractors closing more jobs; one distributor is thousands of contractors. Same vendor logic at scale, and a cleaner conversation than twenty shops (`threads.md` T21).
- **The contractor is also the borrower.** HVAC, plumbing and spray-foam contractors are on Providence's preferred list as *customers*, buying service trucks, recovery machines and rigs. The shop relationship pays twice: he refers his customers, and he is one.

## 5. Where it fits Providence's box, and where it does not

From the preferred and restricted lists (flpool playbook §0.6):

- **In:** HVAC, generators, pumps, plumbing, spray foam, food processing, packaging, concrete, cranes, drilling, water and sewer, towing and recovery, waste. Ticket $40–200K, floor $50K.
- **Out:** restaurants under five locations, medical and dental, gyms, salons, solar. **So the restaurant rooftop-unit case is the right mechanism and the wrong lane.** The same contractor serving a food processor, a cold-storage operator, an ice plant or an industrial customer is in the box.
- **Who is who on the deal.** The borrower is always the owner-operator; the lien is against him, on the unit; the funder pays the seller directly. In the technician case the contractor is the *seller*: he buys the unit from a distributor, marks it up, installs it, invoices the owner. Nothing about the borrower changes. Three questions follow, and they decide the channel:
  1. **Seller acceptance.** Will Providence and its funders accept a licensed service contractor as the seller, rather than a dealer? Lenders vet sellers to stop invoice fraud; a contractor with a licence, an address and a history is usually fine, and commercial HVAC lenders do this routinely. Probably yes; confirm.
  2. **Soft costs.** How much of the financed amount can be installation labor, delivery and commissioning? Lenders cap this, often at a fifth or a quarter. A rooftop-unit install can be a third of the ticket. Lane by lane.
  3. **Collateral.** A vac body on a chassis is titled, movable and repossessable. A rooftop unit or a walk-in rack bolted into a leased building becomes a fixture: a fixture filing in county land records, a landlord waiver, and a unit nobody wants to pull out. This is why truck upfitters rank first and refrigeration second, not a tie. A repair as the ticket has no new collateral at all; that is a working-capital loan and almost certainly outside Providence's box.

## 6. Experiment design

Three candidate lanes, ranked by how much already exists:

| Lane | Contractor | Buyer | Ticket | Why | Where |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Truck upfitters and body shops** | installs vac bodies, knucklebooms, roll-off hoists, service bodies on chassis | specialty-truck operators, the pool we already hold | $50–150K | same lane as every batch so far; the upfitter is seller of record; Providence already finances the asset; David's vendor sheet has three tow/wrecker and one vac vendor of exactly this kind | Florida (pool built), then Texas |
| 2. Commercial refrigeration and HVAC contractors | orders racks, walk-ins, rooftop units, chillers from distributors and installs | food processing, cold storage, ice, industrial (preferred); not restaurants | $40–150K | the residential playbook's nearest commercial cousin; distributors (Johnstone, Ferguson) one level up | Texas or North Carolina (David's best states) |
| 3. Electrical contractors installing standby generators | orders gensets, installs, commissions | water/sewer operators, food processing, industrial | $30–80K | preferred lane; contractor fronts nothing today | same |

**Protocol (H8 in `signals.md`):** twenty shops in one metro. Find them by Google Places category (truck upfitter / truck body shop / commercial refrigeration contractor / generator installer), verify with the state contractor licence where one exists, call the owner. Script: *when a customer's unit isn't worth fixing, what happens next? how often does the job stall because he can't find the money? if you could hand him a monthly number on the spot, and get a finder's fee when it funds, would you put the link on your quote?* Log every answer in the shop register (`signals-execution.md` E-log). Decision rule: five of twenty would use it and one real referred lead within thirty days → build the shop link and partner page; fewer → park with the numbers.

**Prerequisite:** the seller-acceptance answer from David (Saturday), asked plainly: has a deal ever come in through a repair shop, and would Providence take one where the shop is the seller? Run the shop conversations regardless; the answer changes what we promise the shop, not whether the shops are worth talking to.

## 7. Risks and honest weaknesses

- **Repair revenue conflicts with replacement.** An independent diesel shop earns on the repair; recommending replacement is against interest unless it also sells or installs, or the finder's fee is worth more than the repair margin. Refrigeration and HVAC contractors and upfitters do sell and install, which is why the ranked lanes are those.
- **The finder's fee needs a compliance read.** Paying a third party for referring a commercial financing customer is normal in EF vendor programs, but the shop is not a licensed broker; confirm how the fee is structured and disclosed (`threads.md` T12).
- **It is a relationship channel.** Same "hard to reach" shape as vendors. The difference is that nobody else is in the bay, and the ask is small (put a link on the proposal).
- **Seller acceptance, soft costs and fixtures** are in §5; the fixture point is the one that reorders the lanes.
- **Compliance.** The shop is presenting a financing offer to a business; the copy on the shop page and the calculator disclaimer need the same counsel check as the site (`threads.md` T12).
